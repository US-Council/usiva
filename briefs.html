How to use the desk

A source trail is part of the answer.

These briefs explain how to frame a question and where to hand it off. They do not turn public information into a personal determination. Every brief names the official source that supports its orientation and keeps the last-checked date visible.

Read the article that matches the decision in front of you, record the facts that make your situation different, and then verify current requirements with the relevant official agency, school, sponsor, employer, or qualified immigration attorney.

The index / seven desks

Choose the decision you need to make clearer.

Each entry opens a complete long-form brief on the research desk. Use the numbered source notes at the end of each article to inspect the official pages directly.

  1. 01

    Classification desk

    Purpose of travel is the first classification question

    A visa category should follow the real purpose and activities of a trip, not a job title, a hopeful outcome, or a form someone mentioned in passing.

    For
    Readers deciding how to describe a proposed U.S. trip, study plan, research activity, or work question.
    Carry forward
    Write down what you will do, who directs it, who pays, where it happens, how long it lasts, and whether any part looks like study, training, or work before choosing a category to investigate.
    Read the full brief →
  2. 02

    Route map

    Immigrant or nonimmigrant? Draw the route before the form

    The first fork is whether the plan is temporary or intended to lead to permanent residence. From there, location, petition requirements, and the responsible agency change the route.

    For
    Readers comparing a temporary visit, study or work plan with a permanent family- or employment-based immigration objective.
    Carry forward
    Make a one-page route map with the goal, location, petitioner or sponsor, next agency, record number, and unresolved question at each handoff.
    Read the full brief →
  3. 03

    Agency desk map

    USCIS, State, and CBP are different desks

    The immigration system is easier to read when each agency’s decision is named separately: benefit or petition, visa processing, and inspection/admission are not interchangeable.

    For
    Readers unsure which federal agency, portal, notice, or official source should receive the next question.
    Carry forward
    Before you ask for an update, write the agency, decision type, record number, current status, and exact question in one line.
    Read the full brief →
  4. 04

    Evidence desk

    Evidence is a chain of custody for your own facts

    A readiness file is useful when every important claim has a source, date, owner, and question attached to it—not when it is merely large.

    For
    Students, researchers, families, and employers organizing records before a school, agency, sponsor, or attorney review.
    Carry forward
    For each record, write: who issued it, when it was issued, when you checked it, what it supports, what it does not establish, and who owns the next answer.
    Read the full brief →
  5. 05

    Workplace desk

    Employer orientation: Form I-9, E-Verify, and the authorization record

    Form I-9 and E-Verify are employer-side verification processes. They should be coordinated with the worker’s actual immigration and work-authorization record, not used as a shortcut around it.

    For
    Employers, HR teams, international workers, students, and researchers preparing for a compliant hiring handoff.
    Carry forward
    A clean hiring file explains the role, the employee’s Form I-9 process, any E-Verify participation, and the source of the work-authorization information without asking the employer process to decide a visa classification.
    Read the full brief →
  6. 06

    Status desk

    Case status is a handoff map, not an outcome forecast

    The safest status check begins with the agency named on the notice. Use the official portal for that record, preserve the confirmation, and do not convert a status line into a prediction.

    For
    Readers tracking a USCIS filing, a visa process, an immigrant-case handoff, an admission record, or a school/sponsor record.
    Carry forward
    Never start with a search-engine snippet or a third-party tracker. Start with the official notice, agency, record number, and portal named by the agency.
    Read the full brief →
  7. 07

    Data desk

    A small research protocol for official immigration data

    Good immigration research begins with definitions, release context, and source ownership. This brief gives readers a repeatable way to use DHS, Data.gov, USCIS, and State Department data without manufacturing a headline.

    For
    Researchers, policy readers, journalists, students, and practitioners who need a careful starting protocol for public immigration data.
    Carry forward
    Create a research card for every result: question, official source URL, publication or dataset title, release date, filters, measure, denominator, retrieval date, and limitations.
    Read the full brief →

The source protocol

Read broadly. Decide narrowly.

A live official page can change after a brief is published. The date on this desk tells you when the source trail was last checked; it is not a promise that a rule, form, fee, processing estimate, or local instruction will remain unchanged.

  1. 01
    Name the decision.

    Classification, route, agency, evidence, employment, status, or research method.

  2. 02
    Open the owner’s source.

    Use the official page linked in the article, not a copied summary or a search snippet.

  3. 03
    Record the date and fit.

    Note the last checked date, the source’s scope, and the facts it does not answer.

  4. 04
    Verify before acting.

    Ask the relevant agency, school, sponsor, employer, or qualified attorney about current requirements.
